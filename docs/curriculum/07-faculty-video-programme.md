# Faculty Video Programme: Students Ask, Faculty Answer

*Last updated: 25 Oct 2026 · Proposal, not final*

Students submit questions; a faculty member answers one at a whiteboard, on camera, and writes a short accompanying note. The result is a public channel in the style of Numberphile and Computerphile: genuine teaching value, no production overhead, and the best admissions advertising a department can have.

## The idea

Students submit questions. Faculty answer them on camera, at a whiteboard in the faculty room, and publish a short written note alongside each answer.

The format is proven. Numberphile and Computerphile are built on exactly this: one academic, one whiteboard or one desk, one question answered properly, filmed in a single take with no studio and no script. The production value comes from the explanation being good, not from the camera work. Both channels have millions of subscribers and have done more for their departments' visibility than any brochure.

What makes it fit here is that the curriculum already generates the raw material. Students in break-fix drills and workshops produce real questions constantly: why this database, why this failure mode, why this design was wrong. Those are the questions worth filming, and answering them is work faculty already do — the programme only captures it.

## Why it is worth doing

| Who | What they get |
| --- | --- |
| The university | Public evidence of teaching quality, in a form prospective students actually watch. Cheaper and more convincing than advertising, and it compounds: the back catalogue keeps working |
| Faculty | A public record of their teaching, a reason to prepare an explanation properly, and visible credit for work that currently disappears into a classroom |
| Students | Answers they can revisit, and the experience of having their question taken seriously enough to be filmed |
| Everyone else | Free material for other universities and self-taught learners, which is also what gets the channel shared |

There is a second benefit that matters internally. Preparing an answer for a camera forces clarity, and the written notes accumulate into teaching material the department owns: over two years, a library of explanations tied to the curriculum's own topics.

It also pairs naturally with the on-campus AI lab. Both are visible, public-facing investments in capability, and they tell the same story about the department.

## How an episode is made

The loop has to be light enough that a busy faculty member says yes.

1. **Questions come in.** A simple submission page, open to students and eventually to the public. Questions are voted on, so the queue sorts itself.
2. **A faculty member claims one.** They prepare for an hour, not a week. The bar is a good explanation, not a polished lecture.
3. **It is filmed in one take.** Whiteboard, one camera on a tripod, a lapel microphone, the faculty room. Ten to twenty minutes. Mistakes and corrections stay in — that is part of the appeal of the format, not a flaw in it.
4. **A short note is written.** One page: the question, the answer, the diagram from the board, and references. This is the artefact that lasts and that search engines find.
5. **Published together.** The video on the channel, the note on the department's site, each linking to the other and tagged to the curriculum topic it belongs to.
6. **Students run the production.** Filming, editing and publishing are student roles, ideally credited as part of the practice track. The faculty member's only job is the explanation.

A weekly cadence is too ambitious to start. One episode a fortnight, sustained for a year, builds a back catalogue of twenty-five; a weekly schedule that collapses after six weeks builds nothing.

## Cost and what sustains it

**The kit is deliberately minimal.** One camera on a tripod, one lapel microphone, a whiteboard with decent lighting, and free editing software. Spending more is usually a mistake: a studio look is not what makes this format work, and it raises the effort per episode until the programme stops.

**The real cost is faculty time** — roughly two hours per episode, including preparation. That is the number to put in front of the board, not the equipment.

**What kills these programmes** is the schedule, not the budget. Three things protect against it: a named owner, a realistic cadence, and students doing everything except the explaining.

**Start with one department and six episodes.** If they are good, the channel justifies itself and other departments will ask to join. If they are not, nothing has been lost but twelve hours.
